## Project Name

Arescope: Shared Mission Control for Mars Rover Teams

## Summary

Arescope is a shared mission-control prototype for teams of Mars rovers. It combines NASA terrain data, a live 3D fleet map and Guardian, a lightweight ML risk monitor, to help operators understand where rovers are, what they have surveyed and when sensing or mobility begins to degrade.

Our public browser demo coordinates up to six simulated rovers on approximately 40 square kilometres of archived HiRISE terrain. When one rover encounters a simulated wheel obstruction or dusty camera, a healthy neighbour can approach and perform a simulated recovery procedure. More serious failures require Mission Control approval. The affected rover resumes work only after Guardian verifies fresh nominal telemetry.

Incident recording and human-reviewed model updates close the learning loop. We demonstrate the complete workflow in simulation today, with physical rover integration as the next step. The same approach could support terrestrial field robotics where connectivity is limited.

## Project Details

### Problem and purpose

Exploring Mars requires reliable awareness of terrain, vehicle condition and mission progress. Communication delays limit immediate intervention from Earth, while degraded sensing or mobility can interrupt a rover's work. Arescope explores how a team of rovers and its operators can share one operational picture, coordinate assistance and learn from recorded incidents.

### What we built

Arescope LAB is a public browser-based Mission Control prototype. Its interactive 3D map combines archived NASA elevation data with rover positions, survey trails, coverage, risk warnings and mission events. Visitors can start a fleet, choose one to six rovers, change the simulation speed and introduce controlled faults without creating an account.

The fleet divides the survey area into sectors. Guardian evaluates incoming telemetry, including point count, frame rate, position changes, lighting proxies, connectivity and commanded versus observed motion. Mission rules and route search coordinate the response; an LLM does not directly control the simulated wheels.

For minor simulated problems, such as a wheel obstruction, wheel slip or dust on a camera, a healthy rover can approach and perform a timed recovery procedure. Six-wheel models follow the terrain, with wheel and service-arm animation. Serious faults, including sensor dropout or a broken HQ link, require an explicit Mission Control decision. Recovery does not release the halt until fresh nominal Guardian observations confirm it.

LAB also records incidents, supports operator labels, retrains a candidate model and validates it before human-approved activation. This demonstrates a reviewed learning loop rather than unrestricted self-modification.

### NASA data and implementation

We use the HiRISE digital terrain model DTEEC_001488_1750_001752_1750_U02 for a Gale crater strip approximately 9.97 by 4.05 kilometres, and MOLA MEGDR elevation data for regional context. The displayed samples retain source coordinates and documented spacing. Simulated routes and telemetry remain distinct from archived NASA measurements.

The implementation uses Python, FastAPI, SQLite, WebSockets, JavaScript, WebGL, HTML and CSS. A separate Unity/C# and OpenXR project targets Meta Quest 3S as a sensor-mast testbed for future hardware integration; current headset runtime verification remains outstanding. A server-side Voice Copilot prototype explores telemetry-aware voice interaction through OmniRoute.

### Originality, impact and limits

Our contribution is the connected workflow: shared terrain awareness, risk detection, coordinated assistance, operator decisions and reviewed model improvement in one interface. The target architecture has three levels: protection on each rover, coordination across the fleet or an optional local station, and Earth-based oversight and model updates. Guardian inference currently runs in LAB; onboard deployment is a next step.

The current demonstration is an accelerated software simulation on real archived terrain. It does not connect to operational Mars rovers, demonstrate physical repair or establish flight readiness. Orbital terrain samples do not resolve wheel-scale traps. The next practical milestone is testing on a terrestrial rover chassis, with potential applications in field science and remote inspection.

AI tools assisted software implementation, debugging, research, interface design, presentation drafting and demo preparation. Guardian is an ML component, and the optional Copilot uses language models. Generated synthetic telemetry is identified separately from NASA data. The team remains responsible for reviewing and testing the submitted work.
